Style notes

Brutalist (https://brutalistwebsites.com)

Gallery page itself is the style: grey (#EEE) page, monospace default type, blue underlined link, no styling, thumbnails in a plain two-column grid with a text caption. Intro text on the page: brutalism read as a reaction to "lightness, optimism and frivolity of today's web design". First entries visible: RAW Magazine (huge black sans wordmark stacked and overlapped on electric blue, black and white portrait photos), Disslist (white, black outlined nav cells, heavy black headline, black-and-white photo, one neon yellow button). Pattern: raw HTML defaults or oversized grotesques, hard black/white, one clashing colour, overlapping blocks, no softness. Pitfall: easy to look broken; legibility and tap targets still need care. Combines with: editorial (RAW), retro (default fonts), collage.

Pixel art (https://onepagelove.com/style/pixel-art)

One Page Love lists 38 pixel-art one-pagers with a full screenshot and notes each. First cards seen: NODECK (hot pink background, yellow highlighter and sticky note, chunky outlined sticker lettering), a dark terminal-green pixel grid UI (Supabase Select event), a black and white pixel icon sheet (Product Hunt badge). Pattern: pixel type or icons used as a spot accent on an otherwise normal layout is the common move, full 8-bit sites are rarer. Pitfall: pixel fonts at small sizes hurt readability; scale in whole-pixel steps. Combines with: terminal/cyber, playful flat colour.

Retro / Y2K (https://www.webdesignmuseum.org/exhibitions/y2k-aesthetic-in-web-design)

Museum exhibition page (dark navy header, white grid of thumbnails with name and year, 1996-1998 sites first): The Planet 1996 (acid yellow-green, black rounded bars), Durex 1997 (deep blue and orange, round "bubble" buttons), Fullerene Productions 1998 (teal, tiny images), NRG.BE 1998 (grey with an orange chip logo), Spice World 1998 (white, pink and orange round menu), later rows show translucent blue-silver "pill" 3D and glossy gradients. The page's own summary: Y2K web design was "translucent, iridescent, playful and shiny". Pattern: saturated flat panels, rounded/pill controls, glossy chrome gradients, tiny dense type. Combines with: vaporwave, cyber, pixel.

Clay / soft 3D + warm minimal: davesocozy.com (https://davesocozy.com, listed under One Page Love collage tag)

Cream background (#EDE5D3-ish), all type in monospace, glossy olive-green 3D "flower" blob as the one hero object centred in a faint grid panel, tactile dark-brown buttons with soft drop shadow in the corners (SHOP, @DAVESOCOZY, ENTER STUDIO), headline "Hey, I'm davesocozy!" fades in. Pattern: one clay-rendered hero object + monochrome warm palette + chunky pill buttons pinned to viewport corners. Why it works: restraint (one object, two colours) with a tactile material. Pitfall: low contrast while fading in; corner buttons can clash with mobile browser bars. Combines with: minimal, collage, black-restrained rule of one hero object (but cream, not black). Licence: reference only, do not copy assets. enter1997.com: navigation timed out, screenshot blank. Stays. Try timetowarp.com, brutalweb.com next.

Brutalist gallery: Brutal Web (https://www.brutalweb.com)

Flat light-grey page, centred heavy black grotesque wordmark "BRUTAL WEB", underlined text links, three-column thumbnail grid with underlined captions, no cards or shadows. Thumbnails seen: PSP Production (hot magenta, huge white right-aligned caps), Yale School of Art (dense newspaper-like white page, tiny serif, calendar and weather boxes), Heist coffee (royal blue, giant cream grotesque, serif subhead), Snake Height (b/w photo behind stacked bold caps), a black/white geometric logo layout. Pitfall: dense small type (Yale) trades legibility for texture. See also: brutalist row above.

Retro / 1998: TimeToWarp (https://timetowarp.com)

Home page is itself a deliberate 1990s pastiche: white page, Times serif headline "Warp Any Website Back to 1998", navy bordered form box, tiny blue/black underlined links, tab-style grey nav with a "NEW" GIF badge, Windows-98-style grey cookie dialog with blue gradient title bar and bevelled OK button, dog photo logo. The site says it is an AI tool that rewrites any URL into a 1998 GeoCities-style page. Pattern: default serif + hard-blue links + bevelled grey widgets = retro without images. Caveat: it is a generator, so its output is pastiche, not archive; for real 1990s sites use webdesignmuseum.org. Pitfall: cookie dialog gimmick blocks content on small screens.

Aesthetics taxonomy

How to use for builds

  • Austurbane (source) (late 2010s - now): serif fonts, muted colours; black, beige, copper, bronze, gold; large whitespace, "classy" minimalism, zen materials; staged product photography with sharper, more dramatic lighting. Web use: luxury/commercial/resume. Warning: the beige + serif + copper palette is also the AI "warm editorial" wave-2 tell, so derive tones from the project's real materials. [web use/fit/warning: Q author's analysis]
  • Industrial Gothic (source) (late 1970s - late 1990s): dark, moody industrial imagery; wires, cabling, piping, peeling paint; monochrome, moodily lit; a darker variant of Factory Pomo. Web use: horror/film/creative pure black with textured imagery.
  • Nu-Brutalism (source) (early 2010s - now): neo-Swiss/International; heavily contrasting colours, grotesque typefaces, emphasis on the structure of typography. Web use: creative, studio, resume; typographic hero and grid; black/white with one accent.
  • Sportsbrut (source): brutalist, grungy, powerful; simple coarse grids, high-impact type, grunge elements; energy and action. Web use: bold launch/event/film pages; one big type moment.
  • Genericana (source): 1920s-50s packaging, sign-painting fonts, American vernacular, with negative space to denote premium feel and materiality. Web use: craft/commercial brands (food, goods).
  • Paperback Chic (source): natural soft colours, didone or retro display type, airy composition (the "Chobani rebrand" look). Fit: light; usable for resume/editorial, conflicts with dark-first designs unless inverted.
  • Vector Minimalism (source) (2012-late 2010s): flat, quiet, mid-century-referential. Dated now; use only for its restraint.
  • Factory Pomo (source) (late 1980s-early 1990s): clean referential forms, Bauhaus/Constructivism/WPA/industrial revival. Web use: poster-like structured layouts.

Use with caution (high slop risk or off-brand)

  • Cyberbougie (source) (late 2010s-2020s): Corporate Memphis with a cyber twist: monospaced fonts, gradients, black backgrounds, "sleekness" for tech startups. This IS the dark-signal / AI-site default. Avoid as a primary direction.
  • Corporate Memphis (source): 2010s "friendly" corporate: pastel, geometric sans, Matisse-like graphics. The stock illustration-hero look. Avoid.
  • Hexatron (source): 2010s angular sci-fi, neon blue, holograms, terminal UIs.
  • Strong retro/nostalgia or neon/chrome/gloss looks, only for a brief that explicitly wants that era, executed with period-correct typography and imagery: Corporate Synthwave (source), Corporate Vaporwave (source), Laser Grid (source), Cassette Futurism (source), Pulp Fantasy (source), Silicon Dreams (source), Early Cyber (source), Cyberdelia (source), Corporate Cyberspace (source), Y2K (source), Neo-Y2K (source), Vectorheart (source), Frutiger Aero (source), Four Colors (source), Acidgrafix (source), Blob World (source).
  • Notable within that group: Vectorheart pairs Swiss-modernist high-contrast type with utilitarian industrial design (Designers Republic style), a viable edgy base; Frutiger Aero = glossy skeuomorphism, aurora/bokeh photography (do not mix with black-minimal).
  • Whimsicraft (source) (mid 1980s - mid 1990s): folk art and furniture revival; collage, craft, repurposing industrial/found objects; down-to-earth folksy colourful art-fair feel; formerly "DIY Handmade Folk Artsy 80s-90s". Web use: maker/craft marketplaces, kids' brands, community orgs; hand-made texture as hero material. Are.na: https://www.are.na/consumer-aesthetics-research-institute/whimsicraft-knunppjhoda.
  • Pacific Punk Wave (source) (2000-2009): graphic style from punk, pacific wave, Swiss new wave and Italian radical design of the 70s/80s, plus 50s diner/googie/populuxe kitsch (LA novelty store "Heaven"); CARI credits it as a major influence on the 2010s "'80s look" revival. Web use: retro-revival music/fashion pages; treat as a research-pending entry, moodboard via Are.na first. Are.na: https://www.are.na/consumer-aesthetics-research-institute/pacific-punk-wave.
  • Frasurbane (source) (late 1980s - mid 1990s): 90s urbane (named for Frasier's apartment): serif fonts with underlining/italics/weights, warm sepia or black-and-white classical imagery in soft focus, heavily staged product photography, astrolabes/globes/Vitruvian-Man diagrams, Victorian scientific illustrations, textured paper, earthy warm tones, eco-packaging cues (twine, straw, wooden crates). Web use: heritage/craft brands, organic food, boutique hotel; pairs with Paperback Chic. Are.na moodboard: https://www.are.na/consumer-aesthetics-research-institute/frasurbane-rz7lceaf87a.

Pattern takeaways useful across builds

  1. Aesthetics are defined by a bundle: type + palette + materials/imagery + composition + motif. Specify all five, not just a mood word.
  2. Dated-looking = one aesthetic used without a reason; premium = one chosen aesthetic with disciplined restraint.
  3. Nostalgic aesthetics read as authentic only with period-correct detail.
  4. Dark minimal sites cluster in Austurbane / Industrial Gothic / Nu-Brutalism; the AI-default dark look is Cyberbougie.

Glassmorphism

Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/glassmorphism/ (Brown 2024) - Glassmorphism = translucency levels for depth, frosted-glass look; popular since 2020, core to Apple (Vision Pro) and Microsoft (Fluent "Acrylic") systems. Design systems file it under "material". - Two levers: opacity (how much you see through) and background blur (how fuzzy what you see is); strokes/gradients add edge depth, needed over single-colour backgrounds. - Best practices: text over translucent components must still meet contrast (busy backgrounds break it - sample the worst spot); more blur is better over intricate backgrounds (Behance/Dribbble shots blur too little); when the background is out of your control, blur must cover every case; when it is in your control, keep backgrounds simple. - Let users reduce transparency/increase contrast (Apple accessibility precedent). Implementation: backdrop-filter, GPU cost caution .

Josh Comeau: Designing Beautiful Shadows in CSS

Source: https://www.joshwcomeau.com/css/designing-shadows/ (Sep 2021, updated Apr 2026) - Shadows imply elevation; bigger shadow = higher element. Use elevation as an attention tool (dialogs above headers draw focus first). Decide: is this shadow for prominence or for tactility? - One light source per page: every shadow shares the same offset ratio (e.g. vertical always 2x horizontal). Distant-light logic (the sun) makes shared angles correct; per-element angles are campfire logic and unnecessary. - Raise elevation by scaling all four variables in tandem: offsets up, blur radius up, opacity down. - Layered box-shadows (multiple stacked shadows per Tobias Ahlin's technique) are smoother than one big blur; cost = 5x the rendering work per element and don't animate them. - Colour-match: semi-transparent BLACK desaturates the background into washed-out grey; instead take the background's hue, lower lightness (and some saturation) - the shadow reads as a darker version of the surface, not grey film. Too saturated = glow, too desaturated = grey; middle ground is the authentic shadow.

A List Apart: Art Direction and Design

Source: https://alistapart.com/article/art-direction-and-design/ (Dan Mall, Nov 2010) - The distinction that explains why "technically correct" pages can still be emotionally dead: design = technical execution (do the colors match, does the hierarchy work, is the measure comfortable); art direction = the emotion/meaning the technique serves (does it FEEL right for this brand and this audience). The wife-flowers test: the words "I love you" are the art direction; the smile and the roses are the design - mismatched delivery garbles the message. - "Blogazine" trap: a different skin per post is decoration, not art direction. Magazines vary design to enhance each story's MEANING - variation is the result, not the cause. - Poorly designed but well art-directed exists (happy-birthday clip art: execution crimes, but the joyful direction lands). The reverse - well designed, poorly art directed - is the common failure of solo designers art-directing themselves. - Three-hats exercise for generating direction: assignment + design elements + art-direction words (e.g. "dark colors, Swiss type" + "happy, cheerful") - the unusual combinations force invention (how do you make dark colors happy?). - Working split: art direction asks "does this color fit the brand/mood, does the composition's balance convey the right energy (balanced = passive, unbalanced = uneasy/interesting)"; design asks "are the margins even, does the rhythm guide the eye".

Mobile-first UI — touch targets, reach, verified rules

Format: every claim carries a source and verified / unverified.

1. Touch target sizes (three tiers, all verified)

  • WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum), AA: pointer targets at least 24x24 CSS px, with a spacing exception — undersized targets pass if a 24px-diameter circle centered on each target's bounding box does not intersect another target or another undersized target's circle. Four more exceptions: Equivalent (another control does the same), Inline (links in sentences), User agent control, Essential (map pins, dense data viz). Zoom cannot be used to claim compliance; the square must fit fully inside the target, so small circular targets with rounded corners can fail the square test. (source)
  • WCAG SC 2.5.5 Target Size (Enhanced), AAA: 44x44 CSS px, with equivalent/inline/user-agent/essential exceptions. Platform guidance sits in the enhanced tier: Apple HIG gives iOS/iPadOS/watchOS a default control size of 44x44 pt and a minimum of 28x28 pt (macOS 28/20, tvOS 66/56, visionOS 60/28), with spacing treated as seriously as size — about 12 pt padding around bezel elements, 24 pt around bezel-less ones (source). Android/Material recommend at least 48x48 dp, and the touch target extends beyond the visual bounds — a 24dp icon can sit inside a 48dp padded target (Compose Material components do this automatically) (source).
  • NN/g usability minimum for touch: 1cm x 1cm physical (about 0.4in), from Parhi, Karlson & Bederson; physical size, not pixels, because screen densities vary. MIT Touch Lab: average fingertip 1.6-2cm wide, thumb impact area ~2.5cm. Primary CTAs deserve bigger than the minimum; walking/driving contexts demand bigger still. (source)

2. Spacing and error patterns (NN/g)

  • Size first, then spacing: spacing cannot rescue a too-small target (Instagram dismiss-x at 2mm wide fails even with 2mm gaps; 2mm is the often-recommended minimum gap).
  • Stacked thin links/buttons are the classic failure (Kate Spade stacked links); pairing targets side-by-side uses width for error tolerance where line height cannot.
  • View-tap asymmetry: elements big enough to read but too small to tap — the iOS carousel dot indicators are the canonical anti-pattern; desktop-adapted controls (1mm color swatches) fail on touch.

  • Maps: dense small pins cause constant mis-taps; offer a list view as default.

  • Checklist items and filter chips: aim for the AAA 44px row height on checklist rows; chips at >= 32px height with >= 8px gaps.5.8 spacing but 44px is the comfortable target for a phone-first app.

  • Stage cards: make the whole card the tap target rather than a small chevron — kills the view-tap asymmetry problem.

4. How people actually hold phones (Hoober, UX Matters)

(source): - The classic thumb-sweep chart (green arc at bottom, red top corners) is wrong. Thumb range is limited when fingers grasp the phone, but users constantly shift their grip; the Back button at the top gets used all the time. - 75% of users touch the screen only with one thumb; fewer than 50% hold the phone one-handed; 36% cradle it with the second hand for reach and stability; 10% hold in one hand and tap with the other. - People view and touch the CENTER of the screen — not the desktop upper-left-to-lower-right scan, and not a bottom-only thumb zone. Touch accuracy is best at center. - Practical: don't exile key actions to unreachable corners, but don't assume bottom-only either; keep primary interactive content near the vertical center band, and expect grip-shifting.

5. Mobile-first rationale (Wroblewski 2009, the origin text)

(source): mobile-first forces focus — a 320x480 screen leaves no room for extraneous elements, so the team must prioritise the key tasks; mobile capabilities (location, camera, sensors, multi-touch) extend what the product can do beyond desktop. The argument is about constraint-driven focus, not just layout order.

6. Hoober Part 2 — center preference, occlusion, device classes

(source): - People tap where they choose when free: the center half of the screen, on every device class. Put key actions in the middle half to two-thirds; secondary actions and wayfinding along top and bottom edges. - Viewing follows the same center preference: pad the bottom of articles and forms so users can scroll the last line to the middle; avoid full-viewport snap pages for reading content. - Fingers are opaque: a finger fully covers a small icon, so users can't see whether the tap landed until they lift. Leave room around targets so the target and its state change stay visible. - Scrolling happens at the side of the content (far right in LTR, far left in RTL), not necessarily the screen edge — on tablets people scroll nearer the center because content doesn't fill the width. - Device class changes posture: larger device = further from the eyes; ~2/3 of tablet use is on a stand/table/lap; ~10% use a stylus. Adjust type and control sizes per class. Hoober's type-size table (points): small phone body min 4pt/basic 5.5pt, large phone min 6/basic 8.5, small tablet min 8/basic 11.2, large tablet-desktop min 10/basic 14 — derived from angular resolution and viewing distance, not pixel units.

7. Hoober Part 3 — accuracy by zone, interference, affordance

(source): - Touch accuracy is a tolerance problem, not an error problem (R95 circle). Accuracy degrades from 7mm at screen center to 12mm at corners. List rows need 9-10mm height so taps land anywhere along the row; dense content is fine in the center, sparse at edges. - Code the largest possible target: whole rows, boxes and containers tappable, never just the word or icon. Spec it in deliverables; developers should default to whole-area targets. - Interference planning: never place routine controls next to unrecoverable actions (formatting buttons next to Send is his example); separate or provide undo. - People tap only what they see: no gesture-only secret UIs. Box or bound tappable items; keep one affordance per action type — a working trio: selectable rows with arrows (navigate), underlined text (inline info), buttons (submit/change state) covers ~95% of interactions.

Mobile first is NOT mobile only

Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-first-not-mobile-only/ (Budiu & Pernice 2016; 179 users, 6 responsive sites) - Surprising result: navigation used MORE on mobile (63%) than desktop - the twist is the desktop designs were broken, not the mobile ones good. Even on navigation-REQUIRED tasks desktop users found the nav only 54% of the time vs 77% on mobile. - Cause: mobile-first designs ported unchanged to desktop. Offenders: hamburger menus on desktop, nav repeated at page bottom, sticky nav, hamburger in top-right (nonstandard on desktop), search icon instead of a visible search box ("countless users" struggle to find a tiny icon on a 27" screen). - Hidden menus carry vague low-scent labels ("Menu", "Browse"); users ignore them and forget them within the same session. - Rule: same content and functionality on every device, but optimize the LAYOUT and chrome per device class; a design simple enough for a small screen is not automatically good on a large one. Porting unchanged UIs across platforms hurts both.
